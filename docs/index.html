Glossary: what the terms mean
SNP (single-nucleotide polymorphism)
A single position in the DNA where people differ in which letter (A, C, G or T) they have. Your chip measured about 609,424 of these positions, a small fraction of the roughly 3 billion letters in the genome.
rsID
The catalogue number of a SNP in dbSNP, the public database of human variants (e.g. rs429358). Everyone who studies that position uses the same number.
Allele · genotype
An allele is one version of a position (say, C or T). You have two copies of each non-sex chromosome, one from each parent, so your genotype has two letters (e.g. CT).
Reference allele · variant (alt) allele
The reference is the letter in the standard human reference genome. The variant (or "alternate") allele is the other letter. The reference is simply the letter the reference genome happened to contain, not "normal" or "healthy". Occasionally it is the disease version itself (Factor V Leiden is an example).
Heterozygous · homozygous · hemizygous
Heterozygous: two different letters (CT), meaning one copy of the variant. Homozygous: two identical letters (CC or TT). Hemizygous: only one copy exists. Men have one X and one Y chromosome, so genes on them are hemizygous.
Carrier
Someone with one copy of a variant that only causes disease when both copies are affected (a recessive condition). Carriers are usually healthy, but can pass the variant on.
Penetrance
How often people with a variant actually develop the condition. "Low penetrance" means most carriers never do.
ClinVar
The US National Library of Medicine's public database where labs and researchers submit their verdict on what a variant does. This report uses a copy downloaded on 2026-09-24.
Pathogenic · likely pathogenic
Submitters judged the variant to cause (or very probably cause) a disease. It says nothing about how likely you are to be affected: that depends on whether the condition needs one or two copies, on penetrance, and on whether the call is even correct.
Uncertain significance (VUS)
Not enough evidence either way. A VUS should be treated as "unknown", not as a warning.
Benign · likely benign
Judged harmless. Most of your ClinVar matches are in this group, as they are for everyone.
Risk factor · association · protective
Common variants that nudge the odds of a condition up (risk factor) or down (protective), or that turned up in a population study (association). Individually these effects are usually small, and many people with them never develop the condition.
Drug response (pharmacogenomics)
Variants that change how the body processes or responds to a medicine, affecting dose, effect or side effects. Clinically these are read as combinations (star alleles such as CYP2C19*17), not one SNP at a time.
Conflicting classifications
Different submitters to ClinVar disagree about the variant.
Review stars (★)
ClinVar's measure of how much to trust a classification. ★★★★ practice guideline · ★★★ reviewed by an expert panel · ★★ several submitters agree · ★ one submitter who stated their criteria (or submitters conflict) · no stars: no criteria given. Zero-star records deserve very little weight.
Genotyping array (chip)
The technology MyHeritage used. It checks a fixed list of positions and is very accurate for common variants, but it often misreads rare ones. That is why any rare "pathogenic" result needs a clinical test before it means anything.
Call rate · no-call
The share of positions the chip read successfully. A no-call (--) is a position it could not read.
Heterozygosity
The share of positions where your two copies differ. It is a quick sanity check: sex chromosomes in a man should be close to zero.
Runs of homozygosity (ROH) · FROH
Long stretches where both copies are identical because both parents inherited that segment from a shared ancestor. FROH is the fraction of the genome in such runs. Long runs point to recent shared ancestors; short ones are normal background.
GRCh37 · forward strand
GRCh37 (also called hg19) is the version of the reference genome the positions refer to. DNA is double-stranded, and "forward strand" says which of the two strands the letters were read from. Both must match the databases being compared against, and the QC section checks that they do.
Mb (megabase)
One million DNA letters.

Chip artefacts, counted

Of 572,699 measured SNPs also in 1000 Genomes, you are called homozygous for an allele that fewer than 1% of Europeans carry at 3 sites. Hardy–Weinberg predicts about 0.5. 0 of them are alleles not seen in any of the reference Europeans. That is close to what chance predicts, so this file shows no sign of the rare-variant probe errors Weedon et al. (2021, BMJ) describe for consumer arrays (typical for data that did not come from a chip). These calls are flagged in the disease section, masked for pharmacogenomics, and kept out of the imputation used for polygenic scores.

How accurate is the imputation? Measured on 502 people with known answers

The 502 unrelated 1000 Genomes Europeans were cut down to exactly your chip's positions and imputed the same way you were, against a panel that excludes them. 2 · Runs of homozygosity

In plain words: if your parents shared a recent ancestor, you would have long identical stretches (blue bars below) on many chromosomes. Yours are few and short, which is the pattern of unrelated parents.

Long stretches where both chromosome copies are identical are inherited from a shared ancestor on both sides. Many long runs (tens of Mb) point to closely related parents. plink --homozyg with default settings (≥1 Mb, ≥100 SNPs) found 33 segments totalling 48.7 Mb, the longest 5.72 Mb; 9 are ≥1.5 Mb (20.3 Mb). As a fraction of the 2,793 Mb autosomal span the array covers, FROH ≈ 0.0174. No run reaches 10 Mb, the scale close parental relatedness leaves. Short runs like these are typical background, but comparing against a reference population is what would make that quantitative.

3 · Disease and drug-response markers (ClinVar)

In plain words: every person carries thousands of DNA variants listed in ClinVar, and nearly all of them are harmless. What matters for any single line is:

  1. What it is labelled (pathogenic, risk factor, drug response…).
  2. How trustworthy that label is: the review stars. Treat zero stars as a rumour.
  3. How many copies you have (zygosity). One copy of a recessive disease variant makes you a carrier, not a patient.
  4. Whether the chip read it correctly. Chips often misread rare variants (why).

Your 597,939 called SNVs were matched to 4,237,319 ClinVar GRCh37 SNV records by chromosome + position. 28,500 of your sites are in ClinVar, and you carry the ClinVar variant allele at 4,647 records. Most are benign. The array tested 354 sites that ClinVar lists as pathogenic/likely pathogenic with ≥2 review stars. You carry only the reference allele at 353 of them; the exception is flagged below.

5 · Ancestry compared with 1000 Genomes

In plain words: PCA arranges people on a map according to how similar their DNA is. People from the same part of the world cluster together. We draw 2,489 unrelated reference people from 26 world populations, then place you on the same map. ADMIXTURE then estimates what share of your DNA looks most like each continental group.

Limitation that matters here. That tells you where the reference samples are, not where your ancestors lived; section 6 uses a much larger reference.

9 · Polygenic scores

In plain words: a polygenic score adds up thousands to millions of tiny genetic nudges into one number. The percentile says where you rank against a reference group: 70th means your score is higher than 70% of the reference people's. It is relative, not a risk. Someone at the 90th percentile for a condition that affects few people still most likely won't get it, and lifestyle, age and family history also matter.

Read these as a set. With 5 scores, the highest one is expected near the 83rd percentile by chance alone, so one high-looking score among five is not a signal by itself.
